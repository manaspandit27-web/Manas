# Bramblewood Ventures: 2,000 pitches a month

Bramblewood is an early-stage venture fund that invests at pre-seed, seed and Series A. It receives about **2,000 inbound pitches a month** by email and through its website. Two associates read every one and screen it against the fund's written **dealbook**: pass, take a first meeting, or fast-track it to a partner. Each answer carries a reason code, so founders get a consistent explanation and the partners can see where the funnel leaks.

**The problem.** Screening takes an associate about 4 minutes a pitch, which is more than 130 hours a month spent mostly saying no. Speed matters: the best founders run fast processes, and a strong pitch that sits for two weeks is often gone. An audit of last quarter found that **one pitch in six was screened against the wrong rule**. Some were passed on when they should have been fast-tracked. Others went to meetings despite competing with a portfolio company, which the fund has promised its founders it won't back.

**The proposal.** The managing partner wants a model to do first-pass screening, with associates checking only the meetings and fast-tracks. A frontier chatbot with the dealbook pasted in handled the obvious cases, but it missed portfolio conflicts and the sector must-haves. Pasting in the whole dealbook also makes every request several times longer. The operating partner argues for **fine-tuning a small open model (NVIDIA Nemotron) on the fund's own screened pitches**: it would learn the dealbook from examples, run cheaply on the fund's own servers, and keep confidential pitch decks in-house.

**Your job today.** Build the best screening model you can on a fixed budget. You can label pitches yourselves with the dealbook, buy pitches the associates have already screened, and pay for training runs. Then decide whether to keep your labels or pool them with the rest of the class. Where does the model still go wrong, and where must an associate stay in the loop?
